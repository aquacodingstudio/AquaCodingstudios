Shrimp care

Shrimp Health & Warning Signs

Shrimp are small, so health problems can be easy to miss until they become serious. The best defence is not a medicine cabinet — it is stable water, careful observation and noticing when normal behaviour starts to change.

Beginner to intermediate Neocaridina Health

Normal behaviour

Know what healthy shrimp look like first.

Healthy Neocaridina spend much of their time grazing, walking over plants and hardscape, picking at surfaces and occasionally swimming. They may hide more around moulting, after transport or when something in the environment changes.

One quiet shrimp is not automatically a problem. What matters more is whether several animals change behaviour at the same time or whether the change is paired with poor water readings, deaths or visible abnormalities.

Stress signs

Behaviour often changes before anything else is obvious.

Unusual inactivity

Several shrimp sitting still for long periods, especially when they would normally be grazing, can be worth checking.

Frantic swimming

Sudden restless swimming around the tank can occur during normal mating activity, but it can also appear after a water change or parameter shift.

Constant surface gathering

Shrimp repeatedly clustering near the surface may indicate a problem with oxygen, water quality or something irritating the tank.

Loss of appetite

A whole colony suddenly ignoring food is more concerning than one individual choosing to keep grazing elsewhere.

Moulting

A clear shell is not a dead shrimp.

Shrimp must shed their exoskeleton to grow. A normal moult is often transparent, hollow and split behind the head. It may look exactly like a dead shrimp until you look closely.

Leave normal moults in the aquarium unless there is a specific reason to remove them. Shrimp often eat the old exoskeleton and recover some of its minerals.

A moult by itself is normal. Repeated failed moults, multiple deaths around moulting or obvious difficulty escaping the old shell are the things that deserve investigation.

Failed moults

Do not reach for mineral products before checking the whole tank.

Moulting problems can be associated with mineral balance, poor diet, sudden changes in GH or KH, stress, poor water quality or simply an individual animal already in poor condition.

The so-called “white ring” around the body is sometimes discussed as a moulting warning sign, but a pale separation line before a moult is not automatically fatal. Context matters.

Avoid aggressive parameter correction. If hardness appears unsuitable, change it slowly and deliberately. Large mineral or pH swings can create exactly the kind of stress you are trying to solve.

Colour changes

Fading does not always mean disease.

Shrimp colour can vary with genetics, age, stress, substrate, lighting and general condition. A recently transported shrimp may look paler than it did before moving.

What matters is whether fading appears alongside lethargy, poor feeding, abnormal posture, repeated deaths or visible lesions.

Visible abnormalities

Look closely before deciding what you are seeing.

External growths, unusual fuzzy material, damaged limbs, discoloured tissue or abnormal material around the head and underside of the body deserve attention. However, identification from appearance alone can be difficult.

Do not assume every unusual mark is the same parasite or infection. Lighting, eggs, food in the digestive tract and normal anatomy can all look strange if you have not seen them before.

Water quality first

When several shrimp look wrong, test the tank before treating the shrimp.

A colony-wide problem is often environmental. Check ammonia, nitrite, temperature and any other parameters relevant to your setup. Think about what changed recently.

  • Was there a large water change?
  • Was new livestock added?
  • Was the filter cleaned or replaced?
  • Was a medication, fertiliser or chemical added?
  • Could soap, aerosol spray or another contaminant have entered?
  • Was the tank overfed?
The timing of a problem is useful evidence. If shrimp become distressed immediately after a water change or new product is added, that recent change deserves attention.

Sudden deaths

One death and ten deaths are different problems.

Individual shrimp can die from age, failed moults or problems that never affect the rest of the colony. Several deaths close together are much more likely to justify a full water-quality and contamination check.

Remove dead shrimp if they are not being consumed quickly, especially in a small aquarium, because decomposition adds more waste.

Quarantine

New shrimp can bring more than new genetics.

Quarantine gives you time to observe new animals before they join an established colony. It can help reduce the chance of introducing parasites, disease or unwanted hitchhikers.

A quarantine setup should still provide stable water, filtration and basic cover. A tiny unfiltered container is not useful quarantine if the conditions themselves make the shrimp sick.

Treatments

Shrimp are not fish with extra legs.

Many aquarium medications are not designed with shrimp and other invertebrates in mind. Some ingredients that are tolerated by fish can be dangerous to shrimp.

Only treat when you have a reasonable idea what the problem is, and always check whether the product is safe for the species and other invertebrates in the tank.

Do not medicate a whole colony “just in case”. Unnecessary treatment can create additional stress and may damage animals that were never sick in the first place.

Prevention

Boring husbandry prevents a lot of dramatic problems.

  • Use a fully cycled, mature aquarium.
  • Keep ammonia and nitrite at zero.
  • Avoid sudden changes in temperature, pH, GH and KH.
  • Feed sensibly and remove excess food.
  • Use shrimp-safe equipment and treatments.
  • Quarantine new livestock when practical.
  • Observe the colony regularly so changes are noticed early.

Common mistakes

What makes troubleshooting harder.

  • Assuming every moult is a dead shrimp.
  • Changing several water parameters at once.
  • Treating before testing the water.
  • Using fish medication without checking invertebrate safety.
  • Ignoring a colony-wide behaviour change because the water looks clear.
  • Adding new shrimp straight into an established breeding colony without observation.
