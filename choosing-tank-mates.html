Fish care

Choosing Tank Mates

Two species can survive in the same temperature range and still be terrible tank mates. Good compatibility comes from looking at the whole picture: adult size, behaviour, swimming space, social needs, feeding, water chemistry and what each animal is likely to do when the lights go out.

Beginner to intermediate Freshwater Compatibility

Compatibility basics

“Peaceful” is not enough information.

A fish can be peaceful toward its own species and still hunt shrimp, nip long fins, outcompete slower fish for food or become territorial when breeding. Compatibility should be judged by behaviour and husbandry, not by one label on a shop tank.

Think in combinations, not species lists. The same fish may work well in one aquarium and fail badly in another because tank size, group size, layout and individual temperament all change the result.

Adult size

Compare the animals they will become, not the juveniles you are buying.

Many fish are sold young. A species that looks harmless at three centimetres may become large enough to eat smaller tank mates later. Adult body size also affects waste production and the amount of swimming space required.

If one animal can fit another in its mouth, predation is possible. This matters especially with fry, shrimp and very small schooling fish.

Temperament

Watch for aggression, fin-nipping and territorial behaviour.

Territorial fish

Some species defend caves, plants or sections of the tank, particularly when breeding.

Fin-nippers

Active nipping species can be poor companions for long-finned fish such as many domestic bettas.

Predatory fish

A fish does not need to be “aggressive” to eat smaller animals. Hunting can simply be normal feeding behaviour.

Very timid fish

Shy species can be stressed by constantly sharing space with fast, boisterous tank mates.

Social needs

Some species need a group; others need personal space.

Schooling and shoaling fish may become stressed or behave poorly when kept in insufficient numbers. At the opposite end, territorial species may become aggressive when too many individuals are forced into a small space.

Stocking a community tank means providing the correct social setup for every species, not simply fitting one of each into the aquarium.

Water requirements

Tank mates need overlapping conditions.

Temperature, pH, GH and KH should be suitable for all species in the aquarium. A fish that prefers warm, soft water is not an ideal match for a species that requires cooler, hard water just because both are labelled freshwater.

Aim for a stable range that suits everyone rather than constantly adjusting parameters in an attempt to compromise between animals with fundamentally different needs.

Swimming space

Different fish use different parts of the aquarium.

Surface, mid-water and bottom-dwelling species can sometimes share space efficiently, but the tank still needs enough horizontal room, hiding places and open swimming area for each group.

Surface dwellers

Need unobstructed access to the surface and enough room above other active fish.

Mid-water swimmers

Active schooling species often need meaningful horizontal swimming length.

Bottom dwellers

Need usable floor area, appropriate substrate and food that actually reaches them.

Territory users

Caves, wood and planting can help break sight lines and define separate spaces.

Feeding competition

A compatible fish that never gets food is not actually compatible.

Fast, confident fish can dominate feeding time while timid or bottom-dwelling fish miss meals. This is especially common in busy community aquariums.

  • Use foods that reach different levels of the tank.
  • Feed in more than one area when necessary.
  • Watch shy individuals during feeding.
  • Avoid using extra food as the only solution to competition.

Bettas

Betta tank mates depend heavily on the individual fish.

Some bettas tolerate carefully selected tank mates; others do not. Avoid assuming a community setup will work simply because another aquarist succeeded with the same species.

Long-finned, brightly coloured or fin-nipping fish are poor choices. The aquarium also needs enough space and cover to prevent constant interaction.

Always have a separation plan. If chasing, torn fins, hiding or stress becomes persistent, the setup needs to change.

Shrimp

Adult shrimp and baby shrimp are two different compatibility questions.

Some small fish may largely ignore adult Neocaridina while still eating shrimplets. If your aim is to build a breeding colony, a shrimp-only tank offers much better survival.

Dense moss, plants and hardscape can increase cover, but they do not make predation impossible.

Snails

Often easy companions, but still part of the bioload.

Many common freshwater snails coexist peacefully with fish and shrimp. They still produce waste, however, and some species can reproduce rapidly when excess food is available.

Stocking

Compatibility does not override space and filtration.

Even perfectly peaceful species can be a poor combination if the aquarium is overcrowded. More animals mean more waste, more feeding competition and less usable territory.

There is no universal “fish per litre” rule that works across all species. Adult size, body shape, activity, social behaviour and filtration matter too much.

Adding new tank mates

Slow down and watch what happens.

Add new livestock only after confirming the aquarium is stable and suitable for the increased bioload. Quarantine new fish when practical.

Watch closely after introduction. Some aggression appears immediately; other problems only become obvious at feeding time or once territories are established.

Warning signs

Compatibility problems usually leave clues.

  • Persistent chasing.
  • Torn or nipped fins.
  • One fish hiding constantly.
  • A fish being blocked from food.
  • Rapid breathing without a water-quality explanation.
  • Animals being forced into one corner or level of the tank.
  • Repeated predation on shrimp or fry.

Common mistakes

What goes wrong most often.

  • Choosing tank mates based only on similar temperature ranges.
  • Ignoring adult size.
  • Keeping schooling fish in groups that are too small.
  • Mixing slow fish with persistent fin-nippers.
  • Assuming “peaceful” means shrimp-safe.
  • Overstocking because every species is individually compatible.
  • Having no backup tank or separation plan.

A simple checklist

Ask these questions before buying anything.

  • How large will each species become?
  • Do their temperature and water requirements overlap?
  • Are they territorial, predatory or known fin-nippers?
  • Does each species have the correct group size?
  • Is there enough swimming and floor space?
  • Can every animal reach appropriate food?
  • What happens if the combination does not work?
Research every species before bringing it home. A few minutes spent checking adult size, behaviour and water requirements can prevent years of compatibility problems later.
