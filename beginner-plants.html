Aquarium plants

Beginner Aquarium Plants

You do not need injected CO₂, expensive lighting or a shelf full of bottles to grow a good planted aquarium. Starting with hardy species and giving them consistent light, suitable nutrients and a bit of patience is usually the better route.

Beginner Freshwater plants Low-tech friendly

Start simple

Hardy plants give you room to learn.

Some aquarium plants are much more forgiving than others. Beginner species usually tolerate moderate lighting, small mistakes and a wider range of water conditions without immediately falling apart.

Low-tech does not mean no care. Easy plants still need light, nutrients and stable conditions. They are simply less demanding than high-light, CO₂-heavy setups.

Good beginner choices

A few reliable species go a long way.

Java Moss

Hardy, adaptable and useful for shrimp tanks. Attach it to wood, rock or mesh rather than burying it.

Anubias

Slow-growing and tolerant of lower light. Keep the rhizome above the substrate and attach it to hardscape.

Java Fern

Another hardy rhizome plant that does well attached to wood or rock under moderate lighting.

Cryptocoryne

Good rooted plants for low to moderate light. They may melt after being moved but can recover once established.

Vallisneria

A grassy background plant that can spread by runners and fill larger areas once settled.

Salvinia

A floating plant that provides shade and hanging roots. Keep some surface area open for gas exchange and fish access.

Lighting

More light is not automatically better.

Plants need enough light to photosynthesise, but excessive light can encourage algae if nutrients and plant growth are not keeping up.

For many beginner tanks, a moderate light period is a sensible place to start. Adjust based on plant growth and algae rather than trying to copy a fixed number from someone else's aquarium.

Do not solve weak growth by blasting the tank with light. If nutrients are missing or the plant is unsuitable for the setup, more light can simply make the imbalance worse.

Substrate

Rooted plants and rhizome plants are not planted the same way.

Root feeders such as Cryptocoryne and Vallisneria can be planted into substrate. Rhizome plants such as Anubias and Java Fern should not have the rhizome buried, because it can rot.

  • Bury roots, not rhizomes.
  • Attach mosses and rhizome plants to wood or rock.
  • Use root tabs if heavy root feeders need more nutrients.
  • Do not compact the substrate so aggressively that roots struggle to spread.

Fertiliser

Plants need nutrients, but not every tank needs heavy dosing.

Fish waste provides some nutrients, while fertilisers can supply nutrients that are missing. Water-column feeders benefit from liquid fertiliser, while strong root feeders may benefit more from root tabs.

Start conservatively and adjust based on actual plant response. More fertiliser is not automatically better.

CO₂

Useful, but not required for many beginner plants.

Injected CO₂ can increase plant growth and expand the range of species you can keep successfully, but it adds cost and complexity.

Java Moss, Anubias, Java Fern, many Cryptocoryne species and several other easy plants can grow without injected CO₂ when the rest of the setup is suitable.

Plant melt

A plant losing leaves after planting is not always dead.

Some aquarium plants are grown above water before sale. When they are submerged, older leaves may die back while the plant produces new underwater growth.

Cryptocoryne are especially well known for melting after major changes. If the roots and crown remain healthy, the plant may recover.

Algae

Algae usually tells you the system is out of balance somewhere.

New tanks commonly develop some algae. Problems become more persistent when light, nutrients, plant growth and maintenance are badly out of balance.

  • Reduce excessive light duration if needed.
  • Remove badly affected leaves.
  • Keep up with sensible water changes.
  • Avoid overfeeding fish.
  • Give healthy plants time to establish and compete.

Floating plants

Useful shade, but they can take over the surface.

Floating plants such as Salvinia can reduce light, provide cover and create hanging roots for shrimp and fry.

Thin them regularly so they do not block all light from the plants below or prevent fish from reaching the surface.

Shrimp tanks

Plants are especially useful around shrimp.

Mosses and fine-leaved plants provide grazing area, shelter and surfaces for biofilm. Floating roots and dense planting can also help shrimplets avoid predators.

Maintenance

Trim before the tank turns into a hedge.

Remove damaged leaves, thin overcrowded growth and trim plants before they block flow or shade everything underneath.

Healthy trimmings from suitable species can often be replanted or attached elsewhere.

Common mistakes

What trips beginners up most often.

  • Buying high-demand plants for a low-light tank.
  • Burying the rhizome of Anubias or Java Fern.
  • Running the light for very long periods to “help” plants grow.
  • Adding lots of fertiliser without knowing what is missing.
  • Throwing away plants immediately after normal transition melt.
  • Letting floating plants completely cover the surface.
  • Expecting every species to grow quickly in a low-tech setup.

A simple first planted tank

You do not need twenty species.

A very workable beginner setup might use Java Moss on wood, Anubias or Java Fern on rock, a few rooted Cryptocoryne plants, and a small amount of Salvinia at the surface.

That gives you several growth styles without immediately turning the aquarium into a high-maintenance planted tank.
