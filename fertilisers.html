Aquarium plants

Aquarium Fertilisers

Aquarium plants need nutrients, but that does not mean every tank needs heavy dosing. The useful approach is to understand where nutrients come from, what the plants actually use, and whether your setup needs liquid fertiliser, root tabs, both, or neither.

Beginner Freshwater plants Plant nutrition

The basics

Plants need more than light.

Light drives growth, but plants also need carbon and a range of nutrients. Some nutrients come from fish food, waste, tap water and substrate. Others may need to be added deliberately.

Fertiliser is not plant medicine. If lighting, CO₂, substrate or water conditions are unsuitable, adding more nutrients may not fix the real problem.

Macronutrients

Plants use some nutrients in relatively large amounts.

Nitrogen

Commonly available to plants as nitrate in aquariums. Fish waste and feeding can contribute significantly.

Phosphorus

Usually available as phosphate. It is essential for plant growth and energy transfer.

Potassium

Important for normal plant function and often added through comprehensive aquarium fertilisers.

Micronutrients

Small amounts still matter.

Plants also require trace elements such as iron, manganese, boron and others in much smaller quantities. Comprehensive fertilisers often include a balanced mixture of these micronutrients.

More trace elements are not automatically better. Excess dosing is unnecessary and can make troubleshooting harder.

Liquid fertiliser

Useful for plants that feed from the water column.

Mosses, floating plants and many stem plants can access nutrients directly from the water. Liquid fertiliser is therefore a practical way to provide missing nutrients.

A comprehensive fertiliser can simplify dosing by combining several nutrients into one product.

Do not pour in extra because growth looks slow after two days. Plants need time to respond. Large dosing changes make it difficult to tell what is actually helping.

Root tabs

Root feeders benefit from nutrients where their roots can reach them.

Plants such as Cryptocoryne and many heavy root feeders can benefit from fertiliser placed beneath the substrate. Root tabs slowly release nutrients in the root zone.

Root tabs are especially useful when the substrate itself contains little plant nutrition.

Which method?

Match the fertiliser to the plants.

Water-column feeders

Floating plants, mosses and many stem plants often benefit most directly from liquid fertiliser.

Root feeders

Cryptocoryne, swords and similar rooted plants may benefit strongly from root tabs.

Mixed planted tanks

Many aquariums use a moderate liquid fertiliser alongside occasional root tabs for heavy root feeders.

Lightly planted tanks

A tank with only a few slow-growing plants may need very little additional fertiliser.

Fish waste

Livestock already adds nutrients to the system.

Fish food enters the tank and eventually contributes nutrients through waste and decomposition. In a stocked aquarium, nitrate and phosphate may already be present in useful amounts.

This is why two visually similar planted tanks can need very different fertiliser routines.

Shrimp tanks

Dose conservatively and use products suitable for invertebrates.

Planted shrimp tanks can still be fertilised, but avoid unnecessary overdosing and always follow the manufacturer's instructions for the specific product.

Trace amounts of some metals are plant nutrients, but concentrated exposure can be harmful. The word “contains copper” on its own does not tell you whether a correctly dosed fertiliser is unsafe; dose and chemical form matter.

Deficiencies

A damaged leaf does not diagnose one specific nutrient.

Yellowing, holes, pale new growth and stunted plants can be linked to nutrient problems, but similar symptoms can also result from lighting, CO₂, damaged roots, transition melt or poor conditions.

Look for patterns, not one leaf. New growth versus old growth, multiple affected plants and recent changes to the tank can all help narrow down the issue.

Algae

Fertiliser does not automatically cause algae.

Algae problems are usually about imbalance. Excessive lighting, unstable CO₂, weak plant growth, overfeeding and poor maintenance can all contribute.

Completely starving plants of nutrients can make the situation worse by weakening the plants that would otherwise compete for resources.

Dosing

Start with the instructions, then adjust based on the tank.

Use the manufacturer's recommended dose as a sensible starting point. A heavily planted, high-light aquarium may eventually need a different routine from a lightly planted low-tech tank.

  • Measure doses rather than guessing.
  • Keep the routine consistent.
  • Watch new plant growth.
  • Change one part of the routine at a time.
  • Continue normal water changes and maintenance.

Water changes

Fertiliser does not replace maintenance.

Regular water changes help manage dissolved waste and prevent the aquarium from becoming an uncontrolled accumulation of everything being added.

A good fertiliser routine works alongside maintenance, not instead of it.

Common mistakes

What makes plant nutrition harder than it needs to be.

  • Adding extra fertiliser every time a plant looks imperfect.
  • Using root tabs for plants that mainly feed from the water column.
  • Assuming all algae means nutrients are too high.
  • Ignoring strong lighting while reducing fertiliser to almost nothing.
  • Changing several products and doses at once.
  • Expecting fertiliser to compensate for unsuitable light or damaged plants.

A simple beginner approach

Keep the routine boring at first.

For a basic planted aquarium, start with hardy plants, moderate lighting and normal fish or shrimp care. If growth suggests more nutrition is needed, add a suitable comprehensive liquid fertiliser at the recommended rate.

Use root tabs for established root feeders where appropriate. Then watch the aquarium for several weeks before making major changes.
